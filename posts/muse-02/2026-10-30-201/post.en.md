When people choose a color e-reader, many compare screen color first. But the criterion that matters over years of use is not the screen — it is where you buy books and how you read them. A color screen shows comics, magazines and book covers beautifully, but that beauty is useless if books you bought will not open on another device. This post is a pre-purchase introduction based on published materials, not a hands-on review.

> Key line
>
> ### **With a color reader, decide the bookstore, the file format and the library borrowing first — then you will not regret the screen.**

## Both screens are 7-inch color — the differences start after that

Both products use a 7-inch color E Ink screen. Black-and-white text renders at 300 ppi and color at 150 ppi on both, so on manufacturer figures alone it is hard to rank them by screen. According to announcements and comparison coverage (checked 2026-10-30), they diverge on buttons, note-taking and storage. The Kobo Libra Colour has physical page-turn buttons built in and supports a separately sold stylus for notes and underlining, with 32GB of storage. The Kindle Colorsoft (2026) has no buttons on the body — the Signature Edition needs a separate cover for physical buttons — and no stylus support. The base model has 16GB, the Signature Edition 32GB.

## Where you get books is the biggest fork in the road

Kobo reads EPUB files natively, with no conversion. Library borrowing connects through OverDrive built into the device, so books travel without being tied to one store. Kindle does not open EPUB natively — files must go through Send to Kindle conversion — and library borrowing centers on Libby integration for US libraries. On the other hand, if you already bought many books in the Kindle Store or use Kindle Unlimited, the Colorsoft keeps your existing library intact, which is a major advantage. In short, new readers gain from the more open file option, while readers with an existing Amazon library lose less by staying in that ecosystem.

## Three things to check before buying

First, how much comics and magazines you read. For mostly novels, the color screen's benefit is limited to covers and highlights. Second, whether you need to write. If you underline and annotate often, stylus support widens your options. Third, battery figures. Kobo claims up to 40 days and Kindle quotes weeks, but manufacturers measure differently, so do not compare the numbers directly. Treat both as "charge every few weeks" devices. One more note: the second-generation Colorsoft only started shipping on October 28, 2026, so whether its brightness and color improvements are noticeable in practice must wait for independent reviews. If you buy right after launch, treat that point as unconfirmed.

![Illustration symbolizing a personal library and library borrowing](images/03-library.jpg)

An illustration of a personal library connecting with library borrowing. (Illustration) Borrowing availability depends on region and library policy.

The conclusion is simple. If you value buttons, note-taking and file freedom, the Libra Colour is the better deal including price; if the Kindle Store is already part of your life, the Colorsoft is the choice that requires no change. Either way, official prices and sales channels in Korea are not confirmed, so check the seller and warranty terms before buying. This checklist — store, file, library, buttons, notes — works for the next reader you choose, too.
