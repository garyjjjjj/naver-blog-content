When a phone battery wears out, most people replace the whole phone. Repair costs feel high, and finding a repair shop is a hassle. The Fairphone 6 reverses that order. Its battery, screen and charging port are designed for the user to swap. But if you live outside its official sales regions, there are conditions to check first. This post is a pre-purchase introduction based on published materials, not a hands-on review.

> Key line
>
> ### **If replacing a phone just because its battery aged feels wasteful, a repairable phone may be the answer — but check local usability first.**

## What makes this phone different?

The Fairphone 6 is an Android phone from the Dutch company Fairphone. According to the official store (checked 2026-10-29), the Android version costs €599, the deGoogled Murena /e/OS version €649, and refurbished units start at €549. It has 256GB of storage, 8GB of RAM and ships with Android 15. The official store lists eight years of OS and security updates and a five-year warranty. Reports put support until 2033.

An iFixit teardown gives the phone a 10 out of 10 repairability score. The back opens with two T5 screws, and the battery is held by four screws and one cable. With no glue, a battery swap takes about two minutes from shutdown to reboot, according to the teardown. The screen is a 6.31-inch LTPO OLED (10–120Hz) that comes off after eight screws. The USB-C port and camera modules are separately replaceable. The mainboard, however, is not recommended as a user-replaceable part, iFixit notes.

## What should you check to keep it for years?

A repairable phone earns its value through parts prices and support length. First, the battery. Most phone batteries visibly age within two to three years. If you can swap it yourself, you can delay replacing the device by years. Next are the screen and charging port. A cracked screen and a loose charging port are common reasons people replace phones, and both are cheaper to fix when they are separate modules.

![Replacing a smartphone battery illustration](images/02-battery.jpg)

An illustration of a hand replacing a smartphone battery with a small screwdriver. (Illustration) The previous generation was tool-free; the Gen. 6 uses screws.

There are trade-offs. The glue-free design earns an IP55 rating: dust and water-jet resistance, not immersion. That differs from the IP68 rating of flagship phones. iFixit also notes the USB-C port is USB 2 level. Reviews describe the camera and heavy gaming performance as weaker than flagships. In short, this is a practical phone built to last, not a phone chasing peak performance.

## Can you buy it in Korea?

This is the most important part. Official sales in Korea are not confirmed. Murena's shipping list for duty-included delivery covers Europe, the US, the UK, Australia and others, but not Korea. So local network compatibility, VoLTE, radio certification, local service and parts delivery are all unconfirmed. Even if you import one, you should separately check how quickly you could get parts if it breaks.

One more comparison matters right now. Reports say the successor Gen. 6+ launched in August 2026, with a Snapdragon 7s Gen 4 chip, 12GB of RAM, a US price of $649.99 and a European price of €649. The gap to the Gen. 6 (€599) is small. So the sensible condition for choosing the Gen. 6 is a refurbished or discounted price. At full price, comparing the successor first is the better move.

This phone suits people who dislike the cycle of replacing a device whenever its battery ages, and who value a long-lasting structure over top camera performance. If official local service and immediate repair matter most, waiting is safer for now. And if the idea of a repairable phone appeals to you, the checklist in this post — replacement method, support end year, and local usability — works for choosing any other phone too.
