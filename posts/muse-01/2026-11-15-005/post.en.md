## What changes when you actually use it?

**Even if one key breaks, you do not need to replace the whole keyboard.** The CK420 uses hot-swappable switches, so you pull out the broken switch and push a new one in — no soldering. If the typing feel does not suit you, you can swap in a different switch.

A blogger who used the CK420 called it a keyboard where "you can pull out just the broken key and replace it" (jjs5502.tistory.com, hands-on review). For someone who keeps one keyboard for years, that means the cost of one switch instead of a whole new keyboard.

**Blue, red, or brown — pick the switch that fits your hands.** Blue is loud and clicky, red is quiet and light, and brown sits in between. Same keyboard, different feel, just by changing the switches.

The same hands-on review describes the brown switch as having "a reasonable sound and a decent typing feel." If you type late at night, start with red or brown; if you enjoy the click, start with blue.

**The keys stay visible with the lights off, and fast key presses all register in games.** The rainbow LED changes mode and brightness with Fn key combinations, and all 104 keys support full N-key rollover, so keys rarely get dropped mid-game. A 1000 Hz polling rate means about 1 ms response.

The review describes the LED as turning on "sparkling bright" the moment the keyboard powers up. If you like a desk with some color, this one changes the mood of the whole setup.

## The downsides

```blog

path: images/03-img2.jpg

```

**It is not wireless. You plug in a USB cable.** The 1.8 m braided cable has to run behind your desk, so it will never look as clean as a wireless keyboard. On the plus side, there is no battery to charge.

A cable clip behind the desk takes care of most of the mess. There is no wireless mode, so if you want wireless from the start, look at a wireless keyboard instead.

**Pick blue switches and the click gets loud.** Blue switches are built to click, so in a quiet office or late at night, the people around you will hear it. The noise is decided the moment you choose a switch.

If noise worries you, pick red. Already bought blue? Swap in quieter switches — that is exactly what this keyboard is made for.

**A full 104-key layout takes up real desk space.** At 436 × 124 × 36 mm and 993 g, it is clearly bigger and heavier than a tenkeyless board. It is not a keyboard you carry around.

If you rarely use the number pad, a tenkeyless board fits better. If you type a lot of numbers, the full layout pays off — measure your desk and your use first.
